# Pakistan Criminal Procedure Reference

## Source

- Code of Criminal Procedure, 1898 (CrPC), as amended (includes post-18th Amendment provincial procedural amendments in some areas; confirm current provincial-amended text before citing a specific section number as universally applicable).
- Pakistan Penal Code, 1860 (PPC), as amended, including the Criminal Law (Amendment) Ordinance 1990 (which introduced Qisas and Diyat provisions for offences against the human body) and numerous subsequent amendment acts.
- Police Order, 2002 (and provincial Police Acts re-enacted by some provinces after partially reverting from the 2002 Order) — governs police organization and, in part, FIR-related complaint-handling obligations.
- Provincial Criminal Prosecution Service Acts (each province has established/reformed a prosecution service responsible for conducting criminal trials on behalf of the state, distinct from the investigating police).
- Supreme Court and High Court jurisprudence on FIR registration obligations (e.g., rulings affirming that police cannot refuse to register an FIR for a cognizable offence and outlining the complainant's remedy of approaching a magistrate under CrPC Section 22-A/22-B or the High Court where police refuse) — cite specific case names/citations only if confident; otherwise describe the remedy generically.

## FIR Registration (Section 154 CrPC)

- An FIR is recorded by the officer in charge of a police station upon receiving information about the commission of a cognizable offence; the information, if given orally, must be reduced to writing and read over to the informant, who then signs it.
- A copy of the FIR must be supplied free of cost to the informant.
- For a non-cognizable offence, police cannot register an FIR or investigate without the order of a magistrate (Section 155 CrPC); the complainant's remedy is to approach a magistrate directly with a complaint.
- Where police refuse to register an FIR for what is genuinely a cognizable offence, the complainant's remedies include approaching a superior police officer, a magistrate empowered under CrPC (commonly invoked provisions include Section 22-A/22-B, which empower certain magistrates to direct FIR registration, subject to the specific provincial procedural framework), or the relevant High Court through constitutional jurisdiction — hedge the exact provision number if not fully confident, since provincial amendments have altered these provisions.
- An FIR is not proof of the allegations; it is the formal document that triggers a police investigation.

## Cognizable vs. Non-Cognizable, Bailable vs. Non-Bailable

- The First Schedule to the CrPC classifies each PPC offence (and offences under special/local laws generally reference their own schedules or the CrPC framework) along two independent axes:
  - **Cognizable / Non-cognizable**: cognizable offences allow police to arrest without a warrant and to investigate without prior magistrate authorization; non-cognizable offences require magistrate involvement for both arrest (via warrant) and investigation authorization.
  - **Bailable / Non-bailable**: bailable offences entitle the accused to bail as a matter of right, to be granted by the police or the court; non-bailable offences make bail a matter of judicial discretion, guided by the nature/gravity of the offence, the evidence, and other case-specific factors.
- These two classifications are independent of each other — an offence can be cognizable and bailable, cognizable and non-bailable, or non-cognizable, depending on its specific Schedule entry; do not assume one classification implies the other.

## Bail Framework

- **Post-arrest bail**: sought after the accused is in custody; commonly discussed with reference to CrPC Section 497 (bail in cases involving non-bailable offences, with the court weighing factors such as the nature of the accusation, the evidence, and whether the offence carries a specific severe punishment threshold that narrows the court's discretion) and Section 496 (bail in bailable offences, generally a matter of right).
- **Pre-arrest (anticipatory) bail**: sought before arrest by a person apprehending arrest in a non-bailable case; commonly discussed with reference to CrPC Section 498, and typically requires a threshold showing of mala fide, false implication, or similar grounds, since anticipatory bail is treated as an extraordinary remedy rather than a routine one — the specific evidentiary threshold and procedural sequence (e.g., interim bail pending confirmation, appearance requirements) should be verified against current High Court/Supreme Court guidance rather than assumed uniform.
- Bail decisions are made by Magistrates, Sessions Courts, or High Courts depending on the offence and stage of proceedings; a bail grant/denial is generally subject to further appeal/revision to a superior court.

## Investigation Stages to Challan

1. **FIR registration** (Section 154 CrPC) — investigation formally begins.
2. **Investigation** — the investigating officer visits the scene, collects evidence, examines witnesses.
3. **Statements under Section 161 CrPC** — witness statements recorded by police during investigation; these statements are not made on oath and are generally not directly admissible as substantive evidence at trial (used mainly for contradiction/corroboration purposes), a frequently misunderstood point.
4. **Statements under Section 164 CrPC** — statements or confessions recorded before a magistrate, carrying greater evidentiary weight/formality than a Section 161 statement.
5. **Arrest** — may occur at any point where warranted, subject to the cognizable/non-cognizable and applicable arrest-safeguard framework (including constitutional and statutory safeguards on production before a magistrate within a specified period).
6. **Completion of investigation and police report (Section 173 CrPC)** — commonly called a "challan" or charge-sheet in Pakistani practice, submitted to the trial court; it can recommend that the accused be sent up for trial, or (a "cancellation report"/"C-report") that no case is made out and the matter be closed, subject to the complainant's right to challenge a cancellation before the magistrate/court.
7. **Framing of charge and trial** — the trial court frames a formal charge if it finds sufficient grounds, and the criminal trial proceeds (prosecution evidence, defence evidence, arguments, judgment).

## Pakistan Penal Code 1860 — Organizational Structure

- The PPC is organized into chapters grouping offences by subject matter (illustrative categories, not an exhaustive section-by-section list): general explanations/definitions; punishments; general exceptions; offences against the state; offences relating to the armed forces; offences against public tranquility; offences by/relating to public servants; offences relating to elections; contempt-related offences; offences affecting public health, safety, convenience, decency, and morals; offences relating to religion; offences affecting the human body (including homicide, hurt — substantially restructured by the Qisas and Diyat regime introduced via the Criminal Law (Amendment) Ordinance 1990, which incorporated Islamic-law concepts of qisas (retribution) and diyat (compensation) for offences against the body); offences against property (theft, robbery, criminal breach of trust, cheating); offences relating to marriage; offences relating to documents and property marks; criminal intimidation, insult, and annoyance; and attempts to commit offences.
- Specific section numbers and current punishment ranges have been amended multiple times (including through special laws such as anti-terrorism, anti-rape, and honour-crime amendment acts layered alongside or amending the PPC) — cite specific section numbers/punishments only with an explicit "(verify current text)" hedge unless confident.

## Trial-Court Hierarchy (Criminal)

- Magistrates (of different classes, each with differing sentencing-power ceilings) try less serious offences; Sessions Courts try more serious offences, including those carrying the most severe punishments.
- Special courts exist for specific offence categories outside this general hierarchy (e.g., Anti-Terrorism Courts, Accountability Courts for NAB references, and special courts under PECA for cybercrime) — these are outside this skill's general-CrPC scope; route to the relevant specialized skill.
- Appeals from trial-court judgments generally lie to the Sessions Court (from a Magistrate) or the High Court (from a Sessions Court), with further appeal/revision potentially reaching the Supreme Court.

- Always distinguish cognizable/non-cognizable from bailable/non-bailable — these are independent classifications, not synonyms.
- Always distinguish pre-arrest from post-arrest bail.
- Describe PPC structure by chapter/category rather than citing specific section numbers or punishment ranges without a "(verify)" hedge.
- Flag when a matter actually belongs to a specialized pipeline (NAB, PECA/NCCIA, Anti-Terrorism Courts, juvenile justice) rather than describing it under ordinary CrPC/PPC procedure.
